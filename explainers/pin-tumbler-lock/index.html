Pin tumbler lock

A pin tumbler lock is a lock mechanism that uses pins of different lengths to stop the lock from opening unless the correct key is inserted. It is the most common type of lock in door locks and padlocks.

Purpose

A lock prevents one specific movement. In a padlock, that movement is the shackle (the U-shaped bar) being pulled out of the body.

Inside the body, a latch holds the shackle in place. The latch can only be moved by turning the cylinder, and the cylinder can only be turned by the correct key.

Components

A basic padlock has five main parts:

  • Body: the outer case.
  • Shackle: the bar that loops through whatever is being locked.
  • Latch: a bar that fits into a notch in the shackle.
  • Cylinder: the part the key goes into.
  • Pins: small rods inside the cylinder that decide which key will work.

The rest of this article is about the cylinder, where the key is checked.

Cylinder

The cylinder has two parts. The plug is a cylinder that can rotate. The housing (or shell) is fixed and surrounds the plug.

The key goes into a slot in the plug called the keyway. Turning the key turns the plug.

Left: the cylinder cut along its length. Right: the cylinder cut across, seen from the front. Hatching marks cut surfaces.

Shear line

The narrow gap between the plug and the housing is called the shear line.

If nothing crosses the shear line, the plug turns freely. The whole job of the lock is to keep something across this line unless the correct key is present.

Pin stacks

A row of holes, called pin chambers, is drilled down through the housing and into the plug. Each chamber holds a stack of three parts:

  1. Key pin: touches the key. Key pins have different lengths.
  2. Driver pin: sits on top of the key pin. Driver pins are usually all the same length.
  3. Spring: pushes both pins down.

Most household locks have five or six pin stacks.

Locked state

With no key inserted, the springs push each stack down as far as it will go. Every driver pin then sits partly in the housing and partly in the plug, across the shear line.

The driver pins hold the plug to the housing, so the plug cannot turn. A single driver pin across the shear line is enough to block it.

Inserting the key

The top edge of the key has a series of notches, called cuts. As the key slides in, the slopes between the cuts push the pins up, and the springs push them back down.

The pins rise and fall several times before the key is fully inserted.

Bitting

The depths of the cuts are called the bitting. It is usually written as a number, one digit per cut. The key shown here has bitting 3-1-4-2-5, where higher numbers are deeper cuts.

Each cut depth matches the length of the key pin above it. When the key is fully inserted, the top of every key pin lines up exactly with the shear line. The gaps between key pins and driver pins all fall on the shear line at the same time.

Turning

With every gap on the shear line, nothing connects the plug to the housing, and the plug can turn.

The key pins turn with the plug. The driver pins stay in the housing, held up by the outer surface of the plug.

In the lengthwise section, the key appears as a thin line once it has turned, because it is then seen edge-on.

Incorrect key

If any cut is the wrong depth, its pin stack does not line up with the shear line:

  • A cut that is too shallow lifts the key pin across the shear line.
  • A cut that is too deep leaves the driver pin across the shear line.

This key has bitting 3-1-2-2-5. Its third cut is too shallow, so the third key pin blocks the plug. The other four pins are correct, but the lock still does not open.

Releasing the shackle

A cam is fixed to the back of the plug. Here it is a short lever with a pin at its tip. The pin sits in a slot in an arm attached to the latch.

As the plug turns, the cam pin swings sideways and pulls the latch out of the notch in the shackle. A spring then pushes the shackle up. Padlocks use several latch designs, such as ball bearings or spring-loaded bars, but the cylinder works the same way in each.

Manufacturing tolerances

Lock picking is opening a lock without its key by moving the pins directly. It is possible because no lock is made perfectly.

The pin chambers are meant to lie on a straight line, but each hole is drilled slightly off. The errors are only hundredths of a millimetre, but they decide the order in which the pins can be moved.

Right: the cylinder seen from above, cut at the shear line. The offsets are greatly exaggerated.

Tension and binding

A tension wrench is put into the bottom of the keyway and used to apply a light turning force to the plug. The plug turns a tiny amount, until the chamber that is furthest out of line pinches its driver pin between the plug and the housing.

This is the binding pin. The other pins stay loose. Only the binding pin resists being lifted.

Setting a pin

A pick lifts the binding pin. When the gap between its key pin and driver pin reaches the shear line, the plug turns slightly further. The driver pin is now caught on the top of the plug, above the shear line, and the key pin drops back down. The pin is set.

The small extra turn makes a different pin bind.

Single pin picking

The process repeats: find the binding pin, lift it until it sets, then move to the next. This is called single pin picking.

When the last pin sets, nothing crosses the shear line and the plug turns, exactly as it would with the key.

The order is set by the drilling errors, not by the position of the pins.

Raking

Raking uses a pick with a wavy edge, called a rake, which is moved quickly in and out of the keyway while tension is held. The pins bounce up and down, and some set by chance when their gap passes the shear line at the right moment.

Raking is faster than single pin picking but less reliable. It often sets only some of the pins.

Security pins

Many locks use security pins to make picking harder. A spool pin is a driver pin with a narrow waist. When the waist reaches the shear line, the plug turns further than usual. This is a false set: it feels as if a pin has set, but the plug is still blocked.

To get past it, the spool pin is lifted further while the tension is eased, which lets the plug turn back slightly (counter-rotation). Other security pins include serrated pins, which catch at many heights.

Keyspace

The number of different keys a lock design allows is its keyspace. With 5 pins and 5 possible depths there are 55 = 3,125 combinations. Common locks have 5 or 6 pins and between 6 and 10 depths.

The usable number is lower, because some combinations are avoided, such as a very deep cut next to a very shallow one, which would weaken the key. Picking does not depend on the keyspace at all, since it never reproduces the key.

Summary

  • The plug turns only when nothing crosses the shear line.
  • Each chamber holds a key pin, a driver pin and a spring.
  • Without a key, driver pins cross the shear line and block the plug.
  • The correct key lifts every pin stack so that the gap between key pin and driver pin is on the shear line.
  • One wrong cut is enough to keep the lock shut.
  • Turning the plug turns a cam, which moves the latch and frees the shackle.
  • Picking works because the pin chambers are slightly out of line: under tension, one pin binds at a time and can be set.
  • Security pins such as spools add false sets to make picking harder.
