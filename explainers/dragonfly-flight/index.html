How a dragonfly flies

Dragonflies fly with two pairs of wings, each driven by its own muscles. The wings beat back and forth about thirty times a second along a tilted path, flipping over at the end of every stroke. Because each forewing and hindwing can be timed separately, a dragonfly can hover, fly backwards and turn sharply.

This page builds up how a beating wing holds the insect in the air, starting from a flat plate in moving air.

A plate in moving air

The drawing is a cut through one forewing, seen from the side, with the head to the left. The cut face is the chord: the line from the leading edge to the trailing edge.

When the wing moves through air, the air pushes back on it. The push is split into two parts. Lift acts at right angles to the motion; drag acts against the motion.

The wing is drawn still, with the air streaming past it, which gives the same forces.

Angle of attack and stall

The angle between the chord and the direction of motion is the angle of attack. Tilting the wing up turns more of the passing air downwards, and lift grows.

Past about 15° the air can no longer follow the upper surface. It breaks away in a turbulent wake, lift collapses and drag rises. This is a stall. An aircraft wing in steady flight stays well below this angle.

The graph shows the lift of a wing held steady in a stream. Values are illustrative.

Flapping back and forth

A hovering insect has no forward speed, so its wings must make their own airflow. Each wing sweeps forward and back along a stroke plane. The forward sweep is the downstroke, the return the upstroke.

If the wing kept the same tilt on the way back, it would move trailing edge first and push the opposite way. Lift on the downstroke would be cancelled by downward lift on the upstroke, and the net force over one beat would be close to zero.

A horizontal stroke plane is shown first, as used by hovering hummingbirds and many insects.

Flipping at each end

Insects avoid this by rotating each wing at the ends of the stroke. Before the downstroke the wing twists leading edge down, called pronation; before the upstroke it twists the other way, called supination.

The leading edge now leads in both directions, and both half-strokes push the air down. The net force over one beat points straight up.

The faint outlines show the wing at evenly spaced moments of one beat.

The leading-edge vortex

Flapping wings work at angles of attack far beyond the stall angle of a steady wing. As the wing starts each stroke, air flowing round the leading edge rolls up into a swirl above the wing, the leading-edge vortex.

The vortex is a region of low pressure that stays attached to the upper surface for the short length of one stroke, so the wing does not stall. This delayed stall lets a flapping wing make much more force than the same wing held steady.

A real leading-edge vortex is a three-dimensional spiral that runs along the wing. The drawing shows its cross-section.

A tilted stroke

A hovering dragonfly does not beat along a horizontal plane. Its stroke plane is tilted at about 60°, so the downstroke runs down and forward and the upstroke up and back.

The two half-strokes are also different. On the downstroke the wing meets the air at a steep angle, about 60–70°, and pushes hard. On the upstroke it is nearly edge-on and slices through with little force.

Held up by drag

Adding the forces over one beat: the strong downstroke force points up and slightly back, the weak upstroke force points forward, and together they give a net force pointing straight up, balancing the insect's weight.

Because the downstroke runs steeply downwards, much of the upward push is drag, not lift. In a computer model of a hovering dragonfly, drag supplied about three quarters of the upward force. This simple model gives a similar share, shown under the arrows.

Direct flight muscles

The drawing changes to a cross-section through the thorax, seen from the front. Each wing rests on a pivot, the pleural fulcrum, at the top of the side wall.

Muscles attached directly to the wing base pull on either side of the pivot. An elevator muscle, attached inside the pivot, pulls the base down and raises the wing. A depressor muscle, attached outside it, lowers the wing. The pivot works as a lever: a short pull at the base gives a large sweep at the tip.

These direct flight muscles contract once for each nerve impulse. Most other flying insects, such as flies and bees, instead deform the whole thorax with indirect muscles.

Simplified. Each wing has several elevator and depressor muscles, which also twist the wing.

Four wings, four drives

Each of the four wings has its own set of muscles. The nervous system can change the timing, the sweep and the angle of each wing separately.

The dials show where each wing is in its beat. The dragonfly can also beat the left and right wings differently, which is how it turns.

Phase between the pairs

The delay between the hindwings and the forewings is the phase difference, measured in degrees of one beat. The hindwings usually lead.

Hovering dragonflies often beat the pairs almost opposite, about 180° apart. In fast forward flight the hindwings lead by about a quarter of a beat (90°). For take-off and sharp turns the pairs beat nearly together (0°).

The traces show the height of each wing tip over time. Reported values vary between species and studies.

Why the timing matters

The forewing is ahead of the hindwing and slightly above it, so the hindwing beats in the air that the forewing has just pushed. That air is moving down and also rotating.

With the pairs beating opposite, the hindwing can take some of that swirl out of the wake, and in models and experiments the pair then needs less power. Beating together, both wings push at the same time on still air, which gives the most force at a higher cost. Dragonflies choose between economy and power by changing the phase.

The wing itself

A dragonfly wing is a thin membrane stiffened by a network of veins. It is not flat: seen in cross-section it is pleated, which makes it stiff along its length while staying very light. This is corrugation.

The nodus, a joint halfway along the leading edge, lets the outer wing twist a little under load. Near the tip, a thickened, heavier cell, the pterostigma, adds weight at the leading edge. It helps set the wing's twist and keeps the tip from fluttering at high speed.

Summary

  • A wing moving through air feels lift and drag. A steady wing stalls past about 15°.
  • Flapping wings sweep back and forth and flip at each end, so both half-strokes push air down.
  • A leading-edge vortex delays the stall, so flapping wings work at steep angles.
  • A hovering dragonfly tilts its stroke and pushes hard on the downstroke; much of the upward force is drag.
  • Direct muscles drive each of the four wings separately. Changing the phase between the pairs trades economy for power.
