The heart and circulation

The heart is a muscular pump that drives blood around the body through a closed network of vessels, the circulatory system. In an adult at rest it moves about five litres of blood a minute, roughly the body's entire blood volume.

This page follows that flow: how much the heart moves, why the brain is supplied first and most steadily, why blood returning from the feet needs help, and why sitting still for hours is a risk.

Two pumps in one

The heart has four chambers. On each side, a thin-walled atrium collects returning blood and passes it to a thick-walled ventricle, which contracts and pushes it out.

Valves at the entrance and exit of each ventricle let blood pass in one direction only. They are pushed open by forward flow and shut by backward flow.

With each beat, each ventricle pushes out about 70 millilitres. This is the stroke volume.

Schematic front view. As in medical diagrams, the right side of the heart is drawn on the left.

Flow volume

The volume pumped per minute is the cardiac output: stroke volume times heart rate. At rest, 70 mL × 70 beats gives about 4.9 litres a minute.

An adult has about five litres of blood, so the whole volume passes through the heart roughly once a minute. Over a day this adds up to about 7,000 litres and 100,000 beats.

During hard exercise both numbers rise. A fit young adult can reach about 20 litres a minute; trained endurance athletes reach more than 30.

Two circuits in series

The right side of the heart pumps blood through the lungs, where it takes up oxygen. This is the pulmonary circuit. The blood returns to the left side, which pumps it through the rest of the body: the systemic circuit. From the body it returns to the right side.

Because the two circuits are joined end to end, both sides must pump the same volume per minute. If one side pumped more, blood would collect in the other circuit.

Oxygen-rich blood is drawn red and oxygen-poor blood blue, by convention. Real blood is red in both, darker when it carries less oxygen.

Fast in the pipes, slow where it counts

Blood leaves the heart through the aorta, which divides into arteries, then into narrow arterioles, and finally into capillaries: tubes about as wide as one red blood cell, numbering in the billions. They join again into venules and veins.

Each vessel is narrow, but together the capillaries have a cross-section roughly a thousand times that of the aorta. The same volume passes every level each second, about 83 mL, so the blood slows down in proportion: from about 30 cm per second in the aorta to about 0.3 mm per second in a capillary.

The slow passage gives time for oxygen, nutrients and waste to cross the capillaries' thin walls.

Pressure falls along the way

Each beat raises the pressure in the large arteries to about 120 mmHg, and between beats it falls to about 80. This is the familiar blood pressure of 120 over 80.

The arterioles offer the most resistance, and most of the pressure is lost there. The pulsing also fades out. In the capillaries the pressure is about 35 mmHg at the start and 15 at the end; in the large veins it is below 10, and close to zero where they enter the heart.

By the time blood reaches the veins, the push from the heart is almost spent. This matters for the return from the legs.

Where the flow goes

At rest, the five litres a minute are shared out roughly as shown. The kidneys, liver and gut receive large shares because they filter and process the blood itself.

The brain is about 2% of body weight but receives about 15% of the flow and uses about 20% of the oxygen. During exercise, the share going to muscles can rise to more than three quarters, while the brain's supply stays almost the same.

The head's own branches

The aorta rises from the heart and curves over as the aortic arch. Its first branches, the coronary arteries, feed the heart muscle itself. Then three branches leave the top of the arch, heading up.

Two carotid arteries and two vertebral arteries carry blood to the brain. They take the shortest route from the heart, at full pressure, before the rest of the body draws its share.

Inside the skull the four arteries join in a ring, the circle of Willis. If one inlet is blocked, blood can reach its area round the ring. The ring is complete in fewer than half of people, so this protection varies.

Kept steady

The brain stores almost no oxygen or glucose. Without blood flow, a person loses consciousness within about ten seconds, and brain cells begin to die after a few minutes.

The brain's arterioles therefore adjust themselves: they widen when pressure falls and narrow when it rises. This cerebral autoregulation keeps flow close to 750 mL a minute across a range of mean pressures of about 60 to 150 mmHg.

When pressure falls further, the body narrows the vessels to the skin, gut and kidneys, so that more of the remaining flow goes to the brain.

The width of the plateau varies between people and between studies.

Gravity

Blood has weight. In a column of blood, pressure rises by about 0.78 mmHg for each centimetre further down.

Lying down, the head, heart and feet are at about the same height and share the same pressure. Standing up, the brain is about 40 cm above the heart, so the pressure there is about 30 mmHg lower. The feet are about 125 cm below the heart, and the pressure in their arteries is about 100 mmHg higher.

Blood pressure is measured on the upper arm because it is at about the height of the heart.

Soft veins

Gravity alone does not stop blood returning from the feet. Arteries and veins together form a U-shaped tube: blood going down gains as much pressure as blood coming up loses, as in a siphon.

The problem is that vein walls are thin and stretchy. The extra pressure in the lower body inflates the veins, and they fill up with blood that would otherwise return to the heart. On standing up, about 500 to 800 mL shifts into the legs and lower body within a few seconds.

With less blood returning, the next few heartbeats push out less, and the pressure reaching the head drops further.

Sensors at the head's entrance

Pressure sensors called baroreceptors sit in the wall of the carotid sinus, where each carotid artery divides, and in the aortic arch. They measure the pressure of the blood about to enter the head.

When the pressure drops, they signal the brainstem within a beat or two. It raises the heart rate and narrows vessels in the legs and gut. This baroreceptor reflex restores the pressure at the head within about ten to twenty seconds of standing up.

When the reflex is too slow: fainting

Standing up quickly, heat, dehydration, illness or standing still for a long time can overwhelm the reflex. The pressure at the head keeps falling, vision greys out, and the person faints. This is called syncope.

Falling down is a crude but effective correction. Lying flat puts the head level with the heart, gravity no longer works against the supply to the brain, and consciousness usually returns within seconds. Raising the legs helps further.

Soldiers standing still on parade are a classic example: their calf muscles are not working, and blood pools in their legs.

Valves in the veins

The veins of the legs contain one-way valves every few centimetres. Each valve has two thin flaps that point towards the heart. Flow towards the heart pushes them open; flow back towards the foot pushes them shut.

Valves do not lift blood by themselves. Standing still, they stay open, blood creeps slowly upward, and the pressure in the ankle veins is about 90 mmHg, the weight of the full column of blood up to the heart.

What the valves do is make sure that any squeeze on the vein moves blood one way: up.

Close-up of a deep vein in the lower leg. The heart is towards the top.

The calf muscle pump

The deep veins of the leg run between the muscles. When the calf muscle contracts, during a step for example, it squeezes these veins. Blood is pushed up through the valve above, and the valve below shuts.

When the muscle relaxes, the vein refills from below, and the valve above stops blood falling back. Each step pumps a little blood towards the heart. This is the calf muscle pump, sometimes called the second heart.

After a few steps of walking, the pressure in the ankle veins falls from about 90 to about 20–30 mmHg. Breathing helps in the same way: each breath in lowers the pressure in the chest and draws blood into the large veins there.

Leaky valves

If the flaps of a valve no longer meet, because the vein has stretched or an earlier clot damaged it, blood falls back each time the muscle relaxes. The pump loses much of its effect, and the pressure in the leg veins stays high even while walking. This is chronic venous insufficiency.

Veins near the surface then stretch, lengthen and twist: these are varicose veins. The high pressure also pushes fluid out of the capillaries into the surrounding tissue, causing swollen ankles and, in severe cases, skin damage and ulcers.

Sitting for hours

Sitting still, the calf muscle pump is idle. In addition, the veins are bent where the hips and knees are flexed, and the front edge of the seat presses on the back of the thighs.

Flow in the deep leg veins becomes slow. Fluid leaks from the capillaries faster than it is carried away, so the feet and ankles swell; after a long flight, shoes often feel tight.

Swelling is uncomfortable but usually harmless. The greater concern is what slow blood does.

Clots

Blood that moves slowly is more likely to clot. Three conditions favour clotting, known as Virchow's triad (Rudolf Virchow, 1856): slow flow, damage to the vessel wall, and blood that clots more easily than normal, for example after surgery, during pregnancy, with some hormonal contraceptives, with cancer or with some inherited conditions.

A clot in a deep vein of the leg is a deep vein thrombosis (DVT). It often starts in the pocket behind a valve flap. A piece can break off, travel up the vena cava and through the right side of the heart, and lodge in an artery of the lungs. This pulmonary embolism can be fatal.

Travel of more than four hours roughly doubles the risk of a clot. For a healthy traveller the absolute risk stays low, around 1 in 5,000 long flights.

Keeping blood moving

The calf muscle pump does not need walking. Raising the toes and then the heels while seated, so-called ankle pumps, squeezes the deep veins and restarts the flow.

  • Stand up and walk for a few minutes every hour or so.
  • Move the feet and ankles while seated; avoid sitting with legs crossed for long.
  • Drink enough water, since dehydration thickens the blood.
  • People at higher risk may be advised to wear compression stockings, which squeeze the leg from outside.

Summary

  • The heart is two pumps in series, each moving about 5 litres a minute at rest.
  • Blood slows in the capillaries, where the exchange happens, and loses most of its pressure on the way.
  • The brain gets its own branches from the top of the aortic arch, a ring that joins them, and flow held steady by autoregulation and pressure sensors at its entrance.
  • Standing, gravity fills the soft leg veins; if the reflex cannot compensate, a person faints.
  • Valves and the calf muscle pump return blood from the feet. Sitting still turns the pump off, which slows the blood and favours clots.
