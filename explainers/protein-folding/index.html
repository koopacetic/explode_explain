Protein folding

Protein folding is the physical process by which a chain of amino acids, made by the cell as a linear string, takes on a specific three-dimensional shape. That shape decides what the protein does: which molecules it binds, which reactions it speeds up and which signals it passes on.

Knowing the folded shape of a protein lets chemists design drugs that fit it, and explains diseases in which proteins fold wrongly.

A chain of amino acids

A protein is a chain of small molecules called amino acids, joined end to end. Cells use 20 standard amino acids. A typical protein has a few hundred of them; the model here has 48.

The order of the amino acids is copied from a gene. A molecular machine called the ribosome reads a working copy of the gene, the messenger RNA, and adds one amino acid at a time.

Letters are the standard one-letter codes for amino acids. Faint beads have not been added yet.

Water-loving and water-avoiding

Each amino acid has a side chain that gives it its own chemical character. For folding, the most important difference is how the side chain behaves in water.

  • Hydrophobic (water-avoiding) side chains are oily and do not mix with water.
  • Hydrophilic (water-loving) side chains are charged or polar and mix easily with water.

Inside a cell, the new chain is surrounded by water molecules that are constantly moving and bumping into it.

Hydrophobic collapse

Water molecules bond more freely with each other when oily surfaces are hidden from them. As a result, the hydrophobic amino acids gather together on the inside of the chain, and the hydrophilic ones stay on the outside, facing the water.

This hydrophobic collapse is the main force that drives folding. It turns the loose chain into a compact but still irregular ball within a fraction of a second.

Helices and sheets

Within the compact ball, short stretches of the chain settle into regular patterns held together by hydrogen bonds, weak attractions between atoms of the chain's backbone.

  • In an alpha helix, the chain winds into a coil, and each turn bonds to the turn before it.
  • In a beta sheet, two or more straight stretches, called strands, lie side by side and bond to each other.

These patterns are called the secondary structure.

Helices are drawn as ribbons that wind around their axis; strands as flat arrows.

The native fold

The helices and sheets, joined by loops, pack tightly against each other into one specific overall shape. This is the native fold, also called the tertiary structure. A chain with a given sequence folds into the same shape every time.

Many proteins have a pocket: a groove on the surface where another molecule can bind. The pocket is often where the protein does its work.

Sequence determines structure

In experiments on the enzyme ribonuclease around 1960, the American biochemist Christian Anfinsen unfolded the protein with chemicals such as urea. The unfolded protein stopped working. When the chemicals were removed, the chain folded back into its original shape by itself, and it worked again.

This showed that the information needed to fold is contained in the sequence of amino acids. The idea is known as Anfinsen's dogma. Anfinsen shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1972.

Heat also unfolds proteins, which is why a boiled egg white turns solid and does not turn back.

The energy landscape

A chain has a very large number of possible shapes. Testing them one at a time would take far longer than the age of the universe, yet small proteins fold in microseconds to seconds. This contradiction is called Levinthal's paradox (Cyrus Levinthal, 1969).

The usual explanation is an energy landscape shaped like a funnel. Each change that buries more hydrophobic surface or forms more bonds lowers the energy, so the chain moves downhill without searching. The width of the funnel stands for the number of possible shapes, and the bottom is the native fold.

The funnel is a cross-section of a surface with thousands of dimensions, one for each way the chain can bend.

Misfolding

The landscape is not perfectly smooth. A chain can become stuck in a local minimum, a wrong shape from which there is no downhill path. A change of a single amino acid can also make the native fold unstable.

Misfolded proteins expose their hydrophobic parts and tend to stick together. Some stack into long fibres called amyloid, which are found in Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease and several other conditions.

Cells have helper proteins called chaperones that bind stuck chains and give them another chance to fold.

Shape and function

A protein works by binding other molecules. An enzyme, for example, binds a specific partner molecule in its pocket and changes it chemically.

The pocket fits its partner in shape and in the pattern of electric charges on its surface. The fit is often compared to a lock and key, but proteins are flexible: the pocket closes slightly around its partner, which is called induced fit.

Designing a drug to fit

Many drugs are small molecules that block a pocket, so that the natural partner can no longer bind. When the shape of the pocket is known, chemists can design candidates to fit it. This is called structure-based drug design.

  • A molecule that is too large cannot enter.
  • A molecule of the right size with the wrong charges is pushed away.
  • A drug that fits in both ways binds and blocks the pocket.

HIV protease inhibitors, introduced in the mid-1990s, were among the first widely used drugs developed this way.

Predicting structures

Protein shapes are measured with X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance or cryo-electron microscopy. A single structure can take months or years to solve, and many proteins have never been measured.

In 2020 the program AlphaFold 2, from Google DeepMind, predicted structures from the sequence alone, often close to the accuracy of experiments. More than 200 million predicted structures are now freely available. Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work, with David Baker for protein design.

Predictions are less reliable for flexible parts of a protein, and a predicted shape does not show whether a drug will work. They are used as a starting point for experiments, not as a replacement.

Other uses in medicine

Folding matters for medicine in more ways than blocking pockets:

  • Correctors help a misfolded protein reach its native fold. In cystic fibrosis, the most common mutation makes the CFTR protein misfold; corrector drugs such as elexacaftor and tezacaftor let more of it fold and reach the cell surface.
  • Stabilizers hold a protein together. Tafamidis binds transthyretin, a protein made of four parts, and keeps it from coming apart and forming amyloid.
  • Protein medicines such as insulin and antibodies are made by living cells grown in tanks. They only work if they fold correctly, so production is designed around folding.

Summary

  • A protein is made as a chain of amino acids in an order set by a gene.
  • Hydrophobic amino acids gather inside, away from water, and the chain collapses.
  • Hydrogen bonds form helices and sheets, which pack into one native fold.
  • The sequence alone determines the fold, guided down a funnel-shaped energy landscape.
  • Misfolded proteins can clump into amyloid and cause disease.
  • Drugs are designed to fit pockets in the folded shape, or to help a protein fold and stay folded.
